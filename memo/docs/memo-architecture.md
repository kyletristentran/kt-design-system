# Memo architecture (Step 3)

## BLUF — bottom line up front

The first thing the reader sees is the recommendation, not the background. Forty-five words is the ceiling; the verifier enforces it.

A BLUF answers three questions in order: what should we do, what does it cost, why is it defensible.

> **Good.** "Approve $340,000 to open the Westside location. The unit economics clear our bar on every measure that matters, and the model returns capital in 19 months against a five-year IRR of 28.4%."

> **Bad.** "This memo reviews the proposed Westside location and analyzes the financial projections prepared by the operations team, with a recommendation at the end." — describes the memo instead of making the argument.

> **Bad.** "We believe the Westside opportunity may represent an attractive use of capital." — hedged into meaninglessness. If you would not defend it, cut it; if you would, say it.

## The pyramid

Barbara Minto's structure, which is what makes a memo skimmable: a **governing thought** at the apex, **three MECE supports** beneath it, **evidence** under each support. The reader who stops after the BLUF still has the argument. The reader who stops after the three headings has the reasoning.

Test each support with "so what?" — if the answer is a fact rather than a consequence, it belongs in an exhibit, not in a heading.

Section headings state the claim, never the topic:

- Topic (weak): "Unit economics"
- Claim (strong): "The case rests on contribution per service, not on volume"

Three supports is the target. Two reads thin; four means two of them overlap and should be merged.

## Evidence tiers

Mark which tier a figure belongs to, and never blend tiers inside one sentence without saying so.

| Tier | What it is | How to present |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Computed in the source workbook | Bind it as a figure with its cell trace |
| Secondary | External, cited | Name the source inline; it will not have a workbook trace |
| Estimate | An assumption someone made | Say who made it and on what basis, in the exhibit note |

A figure with no defensible tier does not go in the memo.

## Risks

State the objection in the form the strongest sceptic in the room would use, then answer it. Three is usually right.

The tell of a weak risks section is that every risk is already mitigated. If nothing in the plan can actually go wrong, you have not looked. At least one risk should have a response that is a *trigger and a decision*, not a reassurance — "if utilization has not cleared plan by month six, cut marketing and hold" beats "we will monitor utilization closely".

## Memo types and their sections

| Type | Sections after the BLUF | The ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital request** | The economics · The projection and return · The downside | Approve amount, by date |
| **Investment memo** | Thesis · Evidence and valuation · What would make this wrong | Commit / pass / diligence further |
| **Variance explanation** | What moved · Why it moved · What we are doing | Accept the explanation or direct a change |
| **Board update** | Performance against plan · The one thing that changed · What we need from you | Usually informational — say so explicitly |
| **Post-mortem** | What happened · Why it happened · What changes | Approve the remediation and its owner |
| **Vendor recommendation** | The requirement · How the options compare · Why this one | Approve selection and spend |
| **Close package** | Results · Reconciling items · Exceptions and treatment | Sign off on the close |

When the reader is not being asked to decide anything, say so in the ask block rather than inventing a decision. A status report honestly labeled beats a memo pretending to have stakes.

## Length

One to three pages of narrative plus exhibits. If the argument does not fit, the problem is usually that two supports are really one, or that an exhibit is doing work a sentence should do.

No paragraph over five lines. The reader is scanning.
